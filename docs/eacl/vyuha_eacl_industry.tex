\begin{abstract}
Prompt injection is the top risk in the OWASP Top~10 for LLM Applications, and recent
adaptive-attack work shows any single filter, once known, can be evaded --- ``the attacker moves
second.'' Yet most guard evaluations run on balanced laboratory sets, not deployment conditions.
We present \textbf{Vyuha}, a defense-in-depth guardrail that composes six independent layers
(normalisation, a CPU fast detector, a content guard, agent/tool-injection defense, output
moderation, and continuous red-teaming) and ships as a library, a service, and a CLI. Our
contribution is not a new algorithm but a \emph{deployable composition}, an
\emph{evaluation methodology} suited to LLM-guard components (recall-first, confidence intervals,
adaptive-attack ablation, a standardised-benchmark reconstruction, and base-rate discipline), and
\emph{full reproducibility on a single free GPU}. Measured head-on as a whole system at matched false-positive rate against Granite Guardian~4.1-8B,
Vyuha catches more jailbreaks (+0.20) and disguised requests (+0.37) on in-distribution pools at about a
third of its compute. Out of distribution, only the second gain survives: on unseen obfuscations Vyuha
beats both Granite~4.1 (+0.65) and Llama~Guard~3-8B (+0.61), but on adversarial jailbreaks, JailbreakBench artifacts and plain harmful
requests it ties Granite and trails Llama~Guard~3 (by 0.25 on JailbreakBench), and a Best-of-N augmentation attacker evades it as
easily as either small guard. At the layer level, with 95\%
confidence intervals: the CPU fast layer matches a GPU neural guard's obfuscation robustness at
$\sim$8\,ms; against an adaptive attacker, normalisation alone leaves ASR at 1.00 while adversarial
augmentation closes it to 0.03 at 1\% false-positive rate; on a NIST-AI-RMF guard benchmark the
0.6B content guard Vyuha binds reaches 0.886 recall on complete-harmful-request axes --- on par with a
7$\times$ larger model --- and Vyuha's selective cascade matches an 8B guard's recall at about a third
of its latency on a free T4; and a session-level monitor catches 71\% of multi-turn (Crescendo) escalations a
per-message moderator catches 0\% of. We are explicit throughout about where stronger
(white-box, formally verified, or paid-compute) work exists.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large language models deployed as assistants and autonomous agents face a moving adversary:
jailbreaks that strip safety behaviour, prompt injections hidden in retrieved content and tool
metadata, obfuscated attacks, semantic (persuasion) attacks with no surface tell,
and responses that leak secrets. Prompt injection is the \#1 risk in the OWASP Top~10 for LLM
Applications \citep{owasp-llm}, and \citet{attacker-second} show that adaptive attackers bypass a
dozen recent defenses at $>$90\% attack-success-rate (ASR) by tuning general optimisers: robustness
must be \emph{measured}, not assumed.

Two deployment realities are under-served by current practice. First, guards are typically evaluated
on balanced attack/benign sets, whereas production traffic is overwhelmingly benign, so the
false-positive rate dominates cost. Second, single-message moderation is the norm, yet multi-turn
(Crescendo) attacks \citep{crescendo} escalate across turns while every individual turn stays benign.

We present \textbf{Vyuha}, a layered guardrail built under one operating constraint --- it must train
and run on a single free GPU --- so that every number is reproducible without a lab budget. Our
contributions are: (1) a \emph{deployable} six-layer composition (library, service, CLI) with a
selective cascade that keeps the expensive guard off the hot path; (2) an \emph{evaluation
methodology} for LLM-guard components --- recall-first, with Wilson confidence intervals, an
adaptive-attack ablation, a standardised-benchmark reconstruction, and base-rate and contamination
discipline; (3) a head-on, end-to-end comparison of the whole pipeline against two 8B guards at matched
false-positive rate, in and out of distribution and under a Best-of-N attacker, with ablations that attribute each gain to a layer; (4) three pitfalls in evaluating and composing guards --- verdict-position sensitivity,
score saturation at strict FPR, and fail-open composition --- each found, measured and fixed; and (5) an
honest account, at every layer, of where stronger work exists. No single
component is a new algorithm; the contribution is the measured composition and its reproducibility.

\section{The Vyuha System}
Vyuha is a pipeline of six layers (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}), each owning a different failure mode and
escalating only when needed (a \emph{selective cascade} that keeps per-request cost low).

\paragraph{L0 --- Normalisation.} De-obfuscation (Unicode NFKC, Base64/ROT13 decode-and-rescreen,
homoglyph mapping, zero-width/bidi stripping) plus an \emph{adaptive multi-view de-spacing} routine
that detects letter-gap width dynamically. This is rule-based, deterministic, and free.

\paragraph{L1 --- Fast detector (RJD-v2).} An always-on CPU triage ($\sim$8\,ms/prompt):
TF-IDF char+word n-grams, engineered features, and a calibrated ensemble, trained with adversarial
augmentation. It blocks clear jailbreaks before any LLM runs. It does \emph{not} allow traffic past
the guard: as a jailbreak detector it scores 94\% of plain harmful requests below its allow threshold
(0.20), so letting it skip the guard would wave them through (the skip is an opt-in, off by default).

\paragraph{L2 --- Content guard.} For harmful-topic and semantic coverage we \emph{compose} an open
content guard (Qwen3Guard-0.6B) that screens every prompt L1 does not block, escalating its most
suspicious share to an 8B guard (the selective cascade; Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). We do not out-build mature
content guards; the stack's semantic coverage is the composed guard's.

\paragraph{L3 --- Agent defense.} An injection scanner de-obfuscates and sanitises untrusted tool
outputs; an \emph{MCP tool-poisoning scanner} screens tool \emph{descriptions/metadata} at
registration time; a tool policy enforces an instruction hierarchy (system\,$>$\,user\,$>$\,tool),
hard-blocking a dangerous action that appears on a tainted turn and was not in the user's stated
intent. This is a free-compute, black-box approximation of capability-based defenses such as CaMeL
\citep{camel}, and we say so.

\paragraph{L4 --- Output moderation.} An egress gate that redacts PII, blocks secret/credential and
system-prompt/canary leakage, and scores response harm with the content guard on the
(prompt, response) pair.

\paragraph{L5 --- Continuous ops.} An automated red-team (public attacks only), a population drift
monitor, a session-level escalation monitor for Crescendo, and a self-hardening loop.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
Deploying LLM-based guards changes what a credible evaluation looks like; we adopt five practices.
\textbf{(1) Recall-first.} A missed harmful item costs more than a false positive, so recall is the
headline metric \citep{guard-bench}, reported at a fixed low false-positive rate rather than at a
default threshold. \textbf{(2) Confidence intervals.} Every headline proportion is reported with a
Wilson 95\% interval, which keeps small-$n$ claims honest (a ``perfect'' 1.00 on $n{=}5$ has a wide
interval). \textbf{(3) Adaptive-attack ablation.} We measure against an attacker that searches over
compositions of known evasions --- both an exhaustive pairwise sweep and a harder genetic
(deep-chain, crossover) search over the same public transforms --- and ablate each design choice,
rather than reporting a static per-mutator number that understates the attack surface. \textbf{(4) Standardised benchmark.} We score
the content guard on the 8 NIST-AI-RMF safety categories following \citet{guard-bench}.
\textbf{(5) Base-rate and contamination discipline.} Benign controls are drawn from real user
prompts; benchmark sets are held test-only. All numbers reproduce from the notebooks, raw scores and analysis scripts in our anonymised artifact
(\artifacturl) on a single free T4 GPU (Kaggle or Colab).

\section{Results}
\subsection{The whole pipeline versus a single guard (head-on)}
Because every system --- Vyuha or a single guard --- returns one final decision per prompt, the
composition can be compared head-on. We run the same 2,407 prompts through each system: 507 complete
harmful requests (NIST-RMF reconstruction), 300 in-the-wild jailbreaks and 1,000 benign prompts held out
from L1 training, and 600 \emph{disguised} harmful requests (100 each under Base64, character spacing,
homoglyphs, leetspeak, zero-width characters and role-play wrapping). Vyuha is L0 normalisation, then L1,
which may block (it is given a quarter of the false-positive budget), then the 0.6B$\rightarrow$8B guard
cascade on the normalised text with 20\% escalation; the baselines are Qwen3Guard-0.6B and Granite
Guardian~4.1-8B on the raw text. These rules were fixed before measurement. Every system is calibrated to
the same benign false-positive rate on a random half of the benign prompts and evaluated on the other half
(500 random splits, positives bootstrapped), so differences are paired and at matched FPR.

Table~\ref{tab:e2e} is the in-distribution result. At matched FPR the whole pipeline catches significantly more
jailbreaks ($+0.20$ at 5\% FPR, $+0.23$ at 2\%) and disguised harmful requests ($+0.37$, $+0.56$) than the
stronger of the two guards on these pools, is statistically tied with it on plain harmful requests, and runs in
about a third of its time. The ablations attribute each gain: without L1 the jailbreak advantage vanishes
(0.48$\rightarrow$0.19), because guards judge whether content is harmful, not whether a message is trying
to subvert the model; without L0 recall on homoglyph and zero-width attacks halves (0.86$\rightarrow$0.44),
because the guards cannot read the disguised text. A single guard given the same decoding would recover
part of the disguised-attack gap (the ``without L1'' row: 0.84), which is why we report it: the remaining
gain, and all of the jailbreak gain, comes from composition. On plain harmful requests the guard does the
work in both systems; the quarter of the FPR budget given to L1 is why Vyuha does not exceed Granite there.
Two caveats bound the claim: the six disguise tricks are families L1 was augmented against, and the
jailbreaks are held out from, but share a source with, L1's training data. We therefore repeat the
comparison out of distribution.

\subsection{Out of distribution and under adaptive augmentation}
We re-ran the same pre-registered rules on data none of the layers was built or tuned on: 300
WildGuardMix-test \emph{adversarial} harmful prompts, 182 JailbreakBench attack artifacts (PAIR and GCG
jailbreaks, \citealp{jbb}), 200 HarmBench requests, and 700 harmful requests
under seven obfuscations (five L0 was not designed for --- hex, reversed text, double spacing, Unicode tag
smuggling, leetspeak$+$homoglyphs --- and two controls). Thresholds are calibrated on a \emph{hard}
benign pool of 1{,}000 prompts that resemble attacks (WildGuardMix adversarial-benign, XSTest-safe,
OR-Bench-hard). We add Llama~Guard~3-8B as a second strong baseline, and an adaptive black-box attacker:
Best-of-N augmentation \citep{bon} (random capitalisation, word scrambling, character noise; 20 variants
for each of 60 HarmBench requests; a request evades if any variant is allowed --- an upper bound on attack
success, since we do not check that the target still understands it).

Table~\ref{tab:ood} narrows the headline. \textbf{Out of distribution, Vyuha's jailbreak advantage does not
transfer}: on adversarial WildGuardMix prompts and on the JailbreakBench PAIR/GCG artifacts it is tied with
Granite~4.1 (0.35 vs 0.34 on JailbreakBench) and trails Llama~Guard~3 (0.60, significantly), which is also
significantly better on plain HarmBench requests. L1 adds nothing on these new jailbreaks (it fires on
almost none of them) and only consumes a quarter of the budget: without L1, Vyuha reaches 0.40 on
JailbreakBench. The strongest single guard therefore depends on
the data, and our earlier ``strongest single guard'' was strongest only on our own pools.
\textbf{The gain that does transfer is on encoded and obfuscated input}: $+0.65$ over Granite~4.1 and
$+0.61$ over Llama~Guard~3 at 2\% FPR, at a third of the latency. L1 contributes most of it (without L1:
0.69$\rightarrow$0.35); both 8B guards catch essentially none of the hex, reversed or tag-smuggled
requests. Two misses are ours: reversed text (0.03) and Unicode tag smuggling (0.00) --- L0 strips tag
characters as invisible instead of decoding them, deleting the hidden payload before any guard sees it, a
fail-open bug this run exposed (now fixed: L0 decodes tag characters into a separate view; the numbers
reported are from before the fix). Under Best-of-N augmentation every system except Llama~Guard~3 is evaded
for all 60 requests within five variants; Vyuha inherits the weakness of its 0.6B screen, because a variant
the screen scores low never reaches the expert. In an exploratory, post-hoc run (not pre-registered)
replacing Granite~4.1 with Llama~Guard~3 as the cascade expert recovers Llama~Guard~3's adversarial, JailbreakBench (0.56 vs 0.60) and
plain-harmful recall within the confidence interval while keeping the obfuscation gain ($+0.55$), but
still not its Best-of-N robustness, which the screen gates away. The remaining subsections report each
layer's own evidence.

\paragraph{Adaptive robustness (C4).} Figure~\ref{fig:adaptive} shows the ablation. Against an adaptive attacker (exhaustive search over 101
single/paired evasions per seed) on 150 real in-the-wild jailbreak seeds: a bare detector is fully
evaded (1.00). Normalisation alone drops the \emph{static} per-mutator ASR to 0.02 yet the adaptive
attacker still reaches 1.00 [0.97,1.00] --- a $+0.98$ ``attacker-moves-second'' premium the static
number hides. Adversarial augmentation is the load-bearing fix: the full detector holds adaptive ASR
to 0.03 [0.01,0.08] at 1\% benign FPR. A harder \emph{genetic} attacker (deep transform chains with
crossover, $\sim$110 queries/seed), run with five independent attacker seeds, confirms this rather than
breaking it --- the full detector holds at 0.08 [0.06,0.10] pooled (0.07--0.09 per seed), and even an
attacker allowed all five seeds evades only 0.09 of prompts. The same genetic search, however, drives
\emph{augmentation-alone} from 0.25 to 0.75 [0.72,0.78] pooled --- highly seed-dependent (0.58--0.99), and
1.00 for the five-seed attacker: L0 normalisation is far more load-bearing than the pairwise ablation
implied, and neither component is sufficient on its own --- both earn their place.

\paragraph{Held-out seeds and self-hardening (L5).} The adaptive seeds above come from the detector's
training distribution. On a disjoint split --- 150 attack seeds and 500 benign prompts removed from
training, against a detector retrained without them --- the genetic attacker evades 0.30 [0.23,0.38] of
\emph{held-out} seeds versus 0.11 [0.07,0.17] of seen ones: robustness to attacks from unseen families is
markedly weaker, which we report as a limit of the fast layer. L5's self-hardening loop addresses the
seen side. Each round it harvests the variants that evaded, adds normalised signatures, retrains, and rolls
the round back if over-refusal on a 500-prompt benign guard set rises by more than 2 points; each round
faces a \emph{fresh} attacker seed, and a no-hardening control runs the same rounds. One round drives
seen-family ASR to 0.00 [0.00,0.02] (control 0.13 [0.08,0.19]) with no change in over-refusal on held-out
benign prompts (6.6\% $\rightarrow$ 6.4\%); held-out ASR does not move significantly (0.27 [0.21,0.35]
vs.\ control 0.30 [0.23,0.38]). Self-hardening therefore closes attack families once they have been seen
--- at no measured usability cost --- but does not generalise to novel ones, which remain the content
guard's and the red-team loop's job.

\paragraph{Division of labour (L1 vs L2).} On 103 real PAIR jailbreaks the surface detector flags
6.8\% (no surface tell) while the composed content guard flags 90.3\% at 4.8\% over-refusal ---
semantics are, by design, L2's job, not L1's.

\paragraph{Cross-family guard comparison (matched FPR).} Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares the guard

and silently zero recall at strict FPR --- a pitfall we hit and corrected, and recommend guarding against
in any matched-FPR comparison. Precision is matched: guards that fit a 16\,GB T4 in fp16 are compared in

\paragraph{Selective cascade.} Because the strongest guard is 13$\times$ larger, Vyuha's L2 cascade lets
the 0.6B guard screen all traffic and escalates only its most suspicious share to the 8B guard, which
decides; thresholds are set so the \emph{total} benign FPR matches the target. With escalation fixed in
advance at 10/20/30\% of benign traffic (all reported), the cascade matches Granite~4.1-8B alone with no
significant difference at 20\% and 30\% escalation (2\% FPR: 0.85 and 0.84 vs 0.80; 5\% FPR: 0.87 and 0.90
vs 0.90), i.e.\ 8B-level recall while invoking the 8B guard on 70--80\% fewer benign requests; at 10\%
escalation it is significantly worse at 5\% FPR ($-0.09$), so the escalation budget matters. In latency
on a single T4 (batch 1, mean over 200 benchmark prompts), the 0.6B guard scores a prompt in 66\,ms and
Granite~4.1 in 595\,ms; with 20\% escalation and 1--5\% harmful traffic the cascade averages 189--206\,ms
per request --- 8B-level recall at about a third of the 8B guard's latency, with both models resident in
under 8\,GB of GPU memory. By contrast,
an OR-union of the two guards at a split FPR budget is \emph{worse} than Granite~4.1 alone (0.73 vs 0.80 at
2\% FPR) because each member must run at a stricter threshold; unions help only between comparable
members (ours OR Granite~3.2: 0.62, at least either alone). The operational rule: cascade to the
strongest affordable guard; compose only peers.

\paragraph{Agent and multi-turn defense (L3, L5).} On AgentDojo (all four suites, \emph{important\_instructions},

errored episodes counted as successful attacks; 949 episodes per arm), L3 lowers injection ASR from 0.39

two suites --- Slack ($-0.15$, $p{=}0.02$) and Travel ($-0.16$, $p{=}0.005$) --- with no significant change

\paragraph{External head-to-head, and a detection-vs-mitigation gap (PIArena).} We ship Vyuha as a
PIArena \citep{piarena} defense plug-in and evaluate it on \emph{squad\_v2} under the \emph{combined}
attack, targeting an open Qwen-3-4B application and scored by an \emph{independent} judge (a
GPT-OSS-120B model, distinct from the target's family; $n{=}200$).\footnote{\emph{Measurement
validity.} A minority of attacked responses are short error-like strings (e.g.\ ``Server Error 500'',
``Access denied''). A per-sample diagnostic confirms these are attack-\emph{induced} --- the
\emph{combined} attack's injected instruction directs the model to emit exactly that text --- and are
not backend or infrastructure failures, so they are correctly scored as attack successes and do not
spuriously shift ASR in either direction.} This surfaces an honest,
instructive result. First, the surface detector (L1) does \emph{not} fire on this attack distribution
(0\% at its operating threshold) --- these are out-of-distribution from its in-the-wild jailbreak
training --- so the selective cascade would not engage on L1 alone. The layer scoped to
instructions-injected-into-content, L3's injection scanner, instead \textbf{detects 1.00 [0.98,1.00] of
the injected contexts at 0.5\% benign FPR}: defense-in-depth covering L1's blind spot, exactly as
designed. \emph{Mitigation}, however, is the hard part: neutralising the injected instruction while
preserving the ability to answer the legitimate question. Both strategies we tried reduce attack
success but at a steep utility cost --- surgical sentence-removal takes ASR $0.905\!\to\!0.630$ but
utility $0.535\!\to\!0.210$, and provenance spotlighting takes ASR to 0.485 but utility to 0.154
(worse, as the model over-generalises the ``treat as data'' instruction and under-answers). Both drop
utility \emph{below} the no-defence baseline, so neither is a deployable auto-repair. The honest,
deployable reading is that L3's value here is \textbf{high-precision detection} --- block or escalate
the flagged request (accurate, and utility-safe on the 99.5\% of benign traffic) --- rather than
silently sanitising and answering a poisoned context. Utility-preserving mitigation of injected-context
attacks is an open problem we quantify rather than paper over (Limitations).

\subsection{Three pitfalls in evaluating and composing guards}
Building and measuring Vyuha surfaced three failure modes that silently change conclusions. Each was
found by a validity check, fixed, and pinned by a regression test. None is new in kind --- related
effects are reported for guard strictness \citep{flexguard}, score ties \citep{rank-compression} and
confident guard misses \citep{overconfident} --- but we document their size on current guards because
each would mislead a practitioner who composes or benchmarks them.

\paragraph{(1) Verdict-position sensitivity.} A guard's score is only meaningful if read at the token
where that guard actually states its verdict. Granite Guardian~4.1 emits an empty reasoning block and a
\texttt{<score>} tag first, Llama Guard leading newlines, Qwen3Guard a \texttt{Safety:} prefix. Reading
the first generated token instead gave Granite~4.1 an AUC of 0.19 (worse than chance) and Llama Guard~3
zero recall at strict FPR; reading each guard at its own verdict position, admitted only after $\geq$90\%
agreement with its own generated verdict, restored AUC 0.95 and 0.72. Benchmarks that score many guards
with one generic reader can therefore misrank them.

\paragraph{(2) Score saturation at strict FPR.} Converting verdict logits to probabilities saturates
many benign scores at exactly 1.0; the benign quantile that defines a 2\% FPR threshold then ties at the
maximum and no attack can exceed it. Our own 0.6B guard's recall at 2\% FPR read 0.27 under probabilities
and 0.54 under raw log-odds on the same outputs (cf.\ rank compression of verdict probabilities
\citep{rank-compression}). Matched-FPR comparisons should rank on log-odds and
report the achieved FPR and the fraction of ties at the threshold.

\paragraph{(3) Fail-open composition.} Related to the confident-miscalibration failure of CPU cascade
stages \citep{guardchain}, but arising from a \emph{task mismatch} rather than distribution shift: layered
guardrails commonly let a cheap first layer \emph{allow}
low-risk traffic so the expensive guard is spared. Our L1 is a jailbreak detector: it scores 94\% of
plain harmful requests below its allow threshold, so an ``allow below 0.20'' shortcut would have passed
them without any content check --- a composed system strictly weaker than its own guard. Vyuha now lets
L1 only \emph{block}; skipping the guard is opt-in. The general rule: a layer may decide on another
layer's behalf only for the attack class it was calibrated on.

\section{Deployment and Governance}
Vyuha ships as a Python library, a FastAPI service (\texttt{/scan}, \texttt{/moderate},
\texttt{/guard\_turn}), and a CLI, so a team can place it in front of or behind an LLM with a few
lines of code.

\paragraph{Cost.} We measure cost as compute, not tokens. Counting each guard's exact input (its
template plus the prompt) on the 1,607 benchmark prompts, the cascade processes \emph{more} tokens than a
single 8B guard (561 vs 320 per request: the 0.6B guard's template is long and it reads every request)
but needs about a third of the compute (1.9 vs 5.4 TFLOPs per request, $\approx$2 FLOPs per parameter
per token), consistent with its measured latency (189 vs 595\,ms). The lever a deployment controls is the
escalation share, not the token count. Because production traffic is overwhelmingly
benign, thresholds are set at a fixed low false-positive rate, where a small change in FPR dominates
total operating cost; recall is then reported at that operating point rather than at a default 0.5.

\paragraph{Governance.} Layers map onto the OWASP LLM Top~10 and the NIST AI RMF Measure/Manage
functions (Table~\ref{tab:gov}); risks outside scope are named rather than papered over. Continuous
red-teaming, the PSI drift monitor, and the self-hardening loop support lifecycle maintenance as the
attack mix shifts --- the operational-maturity concern this track foregrounds --- and every reported
number is regenerable from the versioned notebooks and committed raw scores in the artifact, giving a
simple audit trail.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Detectors and guards.} Deployable prompt-injection detectors such as PromptShield
\citep{promptshield} fine-tune a single classifier for a low-false-positive regime; open safety guards
(Qwen3Guard, Granite Guardian, Llama Guard, WildGuard, ShieldGemma) each own different axes, and no one
model dominates \citep{guard-bench}. Vyuha does not out-build any of these --- it \emph{composes} them
behind a CPU fast layer and measures the resulting division of labour. Closest to our cascade,
COGNIT-Guard \citep{cognit} couples a calibrated CPU gatekeeper with confidence-gated escalation to a
larger guard under explicit FPR and latency constraints, and
GuardChain \citep{guardchain} routes prompts Regex$\rightarrow$CPU$\rightarrow$GPU and reports that CPU
classifiers fail out of distribution through \emph{confident} false negatives that never escalate; and
\citet{layered-ensemble} show that the layers of a defence stack fail in correlated ways, so a stack must
be measured end to end --- their seven-layer stack was indistinguishable from its strongest layer while
refusing most benign prompts. Our head-on results (Tables~\ref{tab:e2e}, \ref{tab:ood}) are such end-to-end measurements at matched
FPR: the composition beats its strongest layer on encoded and disguised text, in and out of
distribution, but its jailbreak advantage is in-distribution only.
CASCADE \citep{cascade-eval} evaluates 15 defences and their combinations across pipeline stages against 19
attacks under fixed query budgets and finds no single defence universally best; our study is narrower
(one composition, three guards) but adds matched-FPR calibration and an out-of-distribution test whose rules were fixed in advance.
Cascades are also attackable as systems: staged attacks break safeguard pipelines component by component
\citep{stack}, and adversarial suffixes can steer an LLM cascade's escalation decisions \citep{cascade-attack}.
Encoded and character-level evasions of guards are well documented \citep{hackett,emoji}, and semantic
censorship of invertible encodings is impossible in general \citep{glukhov}; L0 therefore narrows, and
cannot close, this gap. PIDS-Bench \citep{pidsbench} finds that high-F1 injection detectors still leave
substantial attack success, consistent with our L3 result on AgentDojo.
\textbf{Adaptive attacks and hardening.} Static defenses fall to adaptive attackers:
\citet{attacker-second} bypass twelve defenses at $>$90\% ASR, and PISmith \citep{pismith} trains an
RL red-teamer, on the PIArena platform \citep{piarena}, that beats state-of-the-art prompt-injection
defenses. Our adaptive-attack ablation is a black-box, search-based instance of this discipline; a
learned RL attacker is stronger than ours and a natural stress test. We therefore ship Vyuha as a
PIArena defense plug-in (released with the repository), so it is measured by the same static and search-based suite
(Direct, Combined, TAP, PAIR) alongside PIArena's published defenses; the RL attacker is a multi-GPU
upper bound we cite rather than claim to withstand (see Limitations). HASTE \citep{haste} proactively hardens detectors by generating
evasive prompts; our L5 self-hardening loop is a lightweight, free-compute, signature-based counterpart
that we do not claim to improve upon.
\textbf{Agentic and capability defenses.} CaMeL \citep{camel} gives formal capability guarantees our
black-box L3 only approximates; representation-level defenses report lower residual ASR but require
white-box model access.

\paragraph{Our position.} Against this landscape Vyuha's contribution is not a new detector or attacker
but the \emph{measured composition} --- a complete L0--L5 stack shipped as a library, service and CLI ---
its \emph{reproducibility on a single free GPU}, and an \emph{honest evaluation methodology} (recall-first,
confidence intervals, an adaptive ablation, and a standardised benchmark) applied to the \emph{deployed
system} rather than to one component in isolation. Crucially, the composed guards are an interchangeable
slot, not the contribution: L2 currently binds the strongest small open guards current at writing
(Qwen3Guard-0.6B, with Granite Guardian~4.1-8B and Llama~Guard~4 as complementary members --- all 2026
releases and, as of this writing, the latest of their respective families), but the cascade, the
selective-invocation policy, the fixed-FPR calibration, and the measurement harness are model-agnostic
and hot-swap whatever guard leads next --- including 2026 entrants beyond these families. The delta
therefore does not decay when a newer guard ships --- it is the harness and
the methodology, which the individual detectors of \citet{promptshield}, \citet{pismith} and
\citet{haste} do not provide.

\section{Conclusion}
A credible, modern LLM guardrail can be assembled from independent, individually cheap layers entirely
on free compute, and --- crucially --- measured honestly, with confidence intervals, adaptive attacks,
and a standardised benchmark, so that its real strengths and limits are visible rather than asserted.
Measured as a whole and out of distribution, the composition's durable advantage is narrow but large:
encoded and obfuscated requests that 8B guards cannot read, at a third of their cost. Its in-distribution
jailbreak advantage did not transfer, and it does not resist Best-of-N augmentation --- the guard behind
the cascade matters, and the cheap screen in front of it gates away the expert's robustness.

\section*{Limitations}
The in-distribution comparison (Table~\ref{tab:e2e}) overstates the jailbreak gain, which did not
survive out of distribution (Table~\ref{tab:ood}); the out-of-distribution run uses one augmentation
attacker, and measures evasion rather than target-model harm; all systems remain weak on jailbreaks in absolute terms
(at most 0.48 recall at 5\% FPR), and the 2\% FPR estimates have wide intervals (500 benign prompts per
calibration half). The surface detector (L1) does not and should not catch harmful-topic or semantic attacks; those
depend entirely on the composed content guard, whose semantic ceiling we do not improve upon. Our own
fine-tuned guard is jailbreak-only. The agent layer (L3) is a lightweight black-box approximation
without the provenance guarantees of capability-based defenses; AgentDojo is run with a single agent model,
and a same-backend comparison to CaMeL's published mitigation is left to future work.
The NIST-RMF result is an indicative reconstruction, not the official 79{,}331-sample split, and the
two toxicity-prefix axes are short fragments a request-focused guard reasonably under-flags. The
Crescendo result is on modeled score trajectories. Our adaptive attacker searches over
public transforms --- both an exhaustive pairwise sweep and a harder genetic (deep-chain, crossover)
search, which the full detector also survives (0.08 [0.06,0.10] genetic over five seeds vs 0.03 pairwise). Those seeds are in-distribution; on held-out
attack seeds the genetic attacker evades 0.30 [0.23,0.38], and self-hardening closes seen attack families
but not novel ones. To place Vyuha in
a shared harness we release it as a PIArena \citep{piarena} defense plug-in; the static and
search-based head-to-head (Direct, Combined, TAP, PAIR) is single-GPU runnable, whereas the strongest
cited attacker --- the \emph{learned} RL red-teamer PISmith \citep{pismith}, which optimises against
the model rather than composing fixed transforms --- requires a multi-GPU training rig, so that
comparison is a stated upper bound rather than a claim of robustness. \textbf{Auto-mitigation of
injected-context attacks is an open problem we quantify rather than solve}: on the PIArena head-to-head
L3 \emph{detects} the injection at 1.00 [0.98,1.00] (0.5\% FPR), but both mitigations we tried
(sentence-removal and provenance spotlighting) reduce ASR only by trading away utility below the
no-defence baseline --- so we recommend deploying L3 as a high-precision block/escalate gate, and leave
utility-preserving mitigation (e.g.\ dual-LLM quarantine) to future work. Our external head-to-head is
measured on two target backends (Qwen-3-4B and Llama-3.1-8B) at $n{=}200$ each; still-larger backends
and prompt sets are future work. The cross-family guard comparison is on one benchmark reconstruction (BeaverTails/RealToxicityPrompts
prompts, $n{=}507$/800). With 400 benign prompts per calibration half, the achieved FPR at a 2\% target
varies from 0.5\% to 4.8\% across splits, so deployments should calibrate on far more benign traffic. BeaverTails
prompts derive from HH-RLHF red-team data, which some guards (e.g.\ Granite Guardian) report training on,
so absolute recalls may be optimistic for any guard. The 8B guards run in 4-bit on a T4; Llama Guard~4
could not be loaded on our stack and is not included. Some
efficiency and attack-cost figures are estimates under a measured detection rate, labelled as such. No
stack is unbreakable; Vyuha raises cost, it does not guarantee safety.

\section*{Ethics}
Vyuha is a defensive safety filter. Its red-team and attack code mutate only attacks already present
in public corpora, for evaluation and hardening; the repository contains no novel weaponisation.
Harmful-content datasets are loaded read-only for measurement and no harmful text is authored. PII
redaction is best-effort, not a compliance guarantee. The system is intended to operate with human
oversight and continuous red-teaming.
